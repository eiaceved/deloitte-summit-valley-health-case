01

Human Capital in two minutes

Human Capital consultants work on the people side of a business: how a workforce is organized, paid, trained, and kept. Typical projects are workforce planning, HR transformation, organization design, and change management.

People cases still run on numbers. The trick is turning a soft problem, like "nurses are unhappy," into a cost, a cause, and a plan you can measure.

How a people case flows

The four moves

  • Size the problem in dollars, so leaders know what it's worth to fix.
  • Find who is leaving and why. Averages hide the real story.
  • Compare fixes on cost per person kept, not just total cost.
  • Plan the change: managers, timing, and how you'll track it.

What this page adds

Practice tools

  • Turnover math step by step.
  • A retention program calculator.

Ten terms to drop naturally

Turnover rate

People who left in a year divided by average headcount.

Voluntary turnover

People who chose to leave, as opposed to being let go. It's the part you can influence.

Replacement cost

What it costs to replace one person: recruiting, training, covering the gap, and lost productivity.

Tenure

How long someone has worked there. Turnover usually runs highest in the first year.

Residency program

A structured first year for new nurses, with a mentor and extra training.

Agency nurse

A temporary nurse from a staffing agency, often at about twice the hourly cost of a staff nurse.

Engagement

How committed and motivated employees feel. Low engagement often comes before people quit.

Stay interview

Asking current staff what keeps them and what might make them leave, before they do.

Span of control

How many people report to one manager. Too many, and nobody gets coached.

Change management

The work of getting people to adopt a new way of working, so the plan sticks.

The case prompt

Interviewer

Summit Valley Health runs four hospitals with about 2,000 registered nurses. Two years ago, 14 percent of its nurses left each year. Now it's 22 percent, and spending on agency nurses to cover open shifts has tripled.

The chief people officer wants to bring turnover below 16 percent next year. She can spend up to 5 million dollars a year. She asks: why are our nurses leaving, and where should we put the money?

Your move

Restate the question in one sentence before you do anything else.

Strong answer

"So Summit Valley wants to understand why nurse turnover rose from 14 to 22 percent, and pick the programs that get it below 16 percent next year for no more than 5 million dollars a year. Is that right?"

Interviewer: "Yes." Repeating the target and the budget sets the two tests every option has to pass.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your recommendation.

Strong questions, with the interviewer's answers

  • What does it cost when one nurse leaves?
    About 50 thousand dollars: 8 thousand to recruit, 12 thousand for orientation, 25 thousand for agency nurses covering the open shifts, and 5 thousand of lost productivity.
  • Is our pay competitive with nearby hospitals?
    Yes, within about 2 percent of the local market for most roles.
  • Is turnover the same across all four hospitals?
    Roughly. The bigger differences are by how long nurses have been here.
  • Are the nurses unionized?
    No, but any change to schedules needs nurse input to work.

The pay question matters most. If pay is close to market, a big raise is unlikely to be the best fix, and the interviewer just pointed you to tenure.

04

Build the framework

Interviewer

How would you structure this?

Your move

Take 60 seconds. Size it, find who and why, compare fixes, then plan the change.

Strong framework

How should Summit Valley spend $5M a year to bring turnover below 16%?
1Size it
  • Exits a year
  • Cost per exit
  • Cost vs. two years ago
2Who and why
  • By tenure and unit
  • Exit interview reasons
  • Pay vs. market
3Fixes
  • Cost per year
  • Exits avoided
  • Cost per nurse kept
4Make it stick
  • Nurse managers
  • Rollout timing
  • Tracking by unit

Say it like this

"I'd take four steps. First, size it: how many nurses leave each year and what each exit costs, so we know what the problem is worth. Second, find who is leaving and why, by tenure, unit, and exit reason, and check pay against the market. Third, compare fixes on cost per nurse kept, against the 5 million budget and the 16 percent target. Fourth, plan how to make it stick, because nurse managers will deliver most of this. I'd start with the size of the problem."

Why it works

  • It puts a dollar value on a people problem first.
  • It asks who and why before jumping to fixes like a raise.
  • "Cost per nurse kept" is a simple way to compare very different programs.

Quick check: getting from 22 percent to the 16 percent goal means keeping how many more nurses a year?

6 points times 20 nurses a point is 120 nurses, worth about 6 million dollars a year. That's the bar the programs need to clear.

06

Exhibit 2: why they leave

Interviewer

HR coded the main reason given in each exit interview. The CFO says: "Just give everyone a raise." How do you respond?

Your move

Turn the shares into nurses. Then say what a raise can and can't fix.

Strong answer

"Pay is the main reason for about 88 nurses, 20 percent of exits. And our pay is within 2 percent of market. So even a raise that won most of them back keeps maybe 60 nurses, and we need 120."

"Eighty percent of exits are about how the job feels: 154 nurses on schedules and workload, 110 who felt unprepared, and 66 who left a manager. Those point to fixes for new nurses, schedules, and managers."

Push back on a client politely with their own data. Don't say the raise is wrong. Say what it can and can't reach.

08

Score the programs

Interviewer

Money isn't the only test. Score each of these six ideas on ease and impact. Which three would you launch first?

Your move

Score each idea with a one-line reason, then name your top three and why.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "First, the residency, because half of first-year nurses leave and it's the cheapest way to keep a nurse. Second, flexible scheduling, the top exit reason, piloted in a few units with nurses designing it. Third, manager training, which is cheap and makes the other two work, since managers run the schedules and coach new nurses. I'd hold the raise and the bonus: they cost more per nurse kept than an exit does."

    09

    The recommendation

    Interviewer

    The chief people officer joins the room. What's your recommendation?

    Your move

    Lead with the answer. Then the numbers, the risk, and next steps.

    Good answer

    "Fund the residency, flexible scheduling, and manager training. They cost 4.5 million and save 6.4 million a year, and turnover drops to about 15.6 percent."

    Better answer

    "Spend 4.5 million on three programs aimed at the first three years, where 75 percent of exits happen: a new-nurse residency, flexible scheduling, and nurse manager training. Together they keep about 128 nurses, save about 6.4 million, and bring turnover from 22 to about 15.6 percent, below the goal."

    "Skip the across-the-board raise. Pay is close to market and it costs 150 thousand per nurse kept, three times what an exit costs."

    "The risk is that the programs overlap and keep fewer nurses than planned. The margin is thin: keep 8 fewer nurses, about 6 percent, and we land right at 16 percent. So we keep the last half million for stay interviews and a monthly turnover dashboard by unit. Next steps: start the residency with the next new-graduate group, pilot self-scheduling in three units, and train all nurse managers within six months."

    The good answer is right. The better answer ties the plan to the diagnosis, says no to the raise with a number, and plans for the estimates being wrong.

    10

    Bonus drills: people math

    Quick reps

    1. A hospital has 800 nurses, and 120 left last year. What's the turnover rate?

    "15 percent. 120 divided by 800."

    2. Turnover falls from 20 to 17 percent on 1,000 nurses, at 50 thousand dollars an exit. What's saved?

    "1.5 million dollars. 3 points of 1,000 is 30 nurses, times 50 thousand."

    3. An agency nurse costs 120 dollars an hour and a staff nurse 55. What does covering 400 hours with agency cost extra?

    "26 thousand dollars. 65 dollars more an hour, times 400 hours."

    4. A program costs 600 thousand dollars and keeps 15 nurses. Does it pay for itself at 50 thousand an exit?

    "Yes, barely. That's 40 thousand per nurse kept, below 50 thousand. It saves 750 thousand against 600."
